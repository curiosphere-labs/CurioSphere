Rani Gaidinliu: The Young Revolutionary Who Challenged British Rule

Rani Gaidinliu joined a socio-religious and political movement as a teenager and became a prominent figure in resistance to British colonial rule in Northeast India. Discover her life, imprisonment and legacy.

By CurioSphere Editorial Team • 2026-10-16 • 6 min read

An account of a young leader from Northeast India whose life became intertwined with community reform, resistance to colonial authority and a long struggle for freedom.

A young leader from Luangkao

Rani Gaidinliu was born on 26 January 1915 in Luangkao village, in the area now associated with Taosem subdivision of Tamenglong district, Manipur. She belonged to the Rongmei Naga community, also referred to in some sources as Kabui. She grew up in a region where colonial administration, local customs, religious life and questions of community self-determination were closely connected.

Gaidinliu’s story is often told as part of India’s freedom struggle, but it is also a story rooted in the history and aspirations of Naga communities in the Northeast. Understanding that setting matters: her activism was connected both to opposition to British rule and to efforts to protect and reshape community religious and social life.

The Heraka movement and Haipou Jadonang

As a teenager, Gaidinliu became associated with her cousin Haipou Jadonang and the Heraka movement. Government of India accounts state that she joined Jadonang’s movement at around the age of 13, in 1927. The movement had religious, social and political dimensions. It sought to revive or reform Naga religious practices and promoted self-rule, while also opposing aspects of British colonial control.

The movement developed amid wider tensions over colonial administration, taxation, forced labour and land use. Historical scholarship has also examined the movement in relation to agricultural pressures, British land policy and changes to social and religious life. These factors help explain why it cannot be reduced to a single issue or described only as a conventional nationalist organisation.

Jadonang became a central figure in the movement. British authorities executed him in 1931. After his death, Gaidinliu continued the movement’s work and emerged as a leader in resistance to colonial authority.

Resistance and arrest

Gaidinliu’s activities brought her into direct conflict with British authorities. Accounts describe her followers organising resistance and refusing to accept colonial demands. In 1932, when she was 16, she was arrested by British forces.

The circumstances of her imprisonment require careful wording. Official Indian commemorative profiles commonly summarise the episode by saying that she was arrested in 1932 and sentenced to life imprisonment by the British. A more detailed historical account published by the Press Information Bureau describes a trial lasting about ten months and says that she was convicted on charges of murder and abetment of murder, with the life sentence imposed for abetment of murder. That account links the case to violence involving one of her followers’ communities after her arrest.

It would therefore be misleading to say simply that a court sentenced her to life imprisonment solely for “fighting for freedom.” Her imprisonment occurred in the context of colonial repression of a movement challenging British authority, but the documented formal conviction and the wider political context are distinct parts of the history. The available summary sources do not provide enough material to independently reassess the trial evidence or the fairness of the proceedings, so this article does not attempt to do so.

Fourteen years in prison

Following her conviction, Gaidinliu was held in several prisons in the Northeast. Government of India sources state that she spent approximately 14 years in prison and was released in 1947, after India achieved independence. Her long imprisonment became one of the defining features of her public legacy.

Her release did not mean that her life’s work was over. Accounts from official commemorative sources say that she continued to work for the welfare of her community after leaving prison. Her story remained closely connected to the history, identity and future of the people among whom she had first become a leader.

Why was she called “Rani”?

The title “Rani” became associated with Gaidinliu because of her courage and leadership. The Nehru Archive preserves historical correspondence and records referring to Jawaharlal Nehru’s interest in her case and his efforts to seek her release. Later accounts commonly report that Nehru called her the “Daughter of the Hills” and helped popularise the title “Rani.”

These expressions became part of how she was remembered nationally. They should not obscure the fact that Gaidinliu’s leadership first arose from a specific local and regional context in the Northeast, where the Heraka movement addressed religious, social and political questions as well as opposition to colonial rule.

Recognition and legacy

After independence, Gaidinliu continued to be recognised for her role in resistance to British rule and for her work among her people. She received the Padma Bhushan in 1982. She died on 17 February 1993 in her native village of Luangkao, according to the Ministry of Tribal Affairs’ account published by the Press Information Bureau.

Her legacy is significant for several reasons. She was a young woman who took on leadership in a period of intense colonial conflict; her story brings attention to the varied histories of resistance in Northeast India; and her life shows how movements for self-determination can be shaped by religious, cultural, social and political concerns at the same time.

Remembering Gaidinliu responsibly means recognising her contribution without flattening the complexity of the movement she led or presenting contested historical details as settled fact. Her story belongs within the larger history of India’s struggle against colonial rule, while also deserving attention as part of the distinct histories of the Naga communities of the Northeast.

Key facts at a glance

  • Born: 26 January 1915, Luangkao village, in present-day Manipur.
  • Movement: Associated as a teenager with Haipou Jadonang and the Heraka movement.
  • Leadership: Continued the movement after Jadonang’s execution in 1931.
  • Arrest: Arrested in 1932, aged 16.
  • Imprisonment: Sentenced to life imprisonment; official Indian accounts describe approximately 14 years in prison.
  • Release: 1947.
  • Award: Padma Bhushan, 1982.
  • Died: 17 February 1993, at Luangkao.
